# How we Make the National Zoning Atlas

Zoning is decentralized, inconsistent, and convoluted. Our [team](https://www.zoningatlas.org/team) follows 4 basic steps to make key information about zoning centralized, standardized, and accessible.

## step 1: IDENTIFY JURISDICTIONS

Building the National Zoning Atlas requires a complete list of **_jurisdictions_**: cities, towns, counties, villages, and other places that can exercise zoning.

### WE’VE identified 33,295 jurisdictions, with more to come.

We add to the master jurisdiction file when new local governments are created. Or when we find private associations or other special entities that have zoning authority.

State legislatures decide how many jurisdictions can zone through enabling statutes. Hawaii gives only its 5 counties (not cities or towns) the zoning power. Minnesota, meanwhile, tops our charts with 2,650 potential zoning jurisdictions.

Colorado is an example in the middle. It has 334 jurisdictions, and 59 have declined to exercise their zoning authority. Here are a few Colorado jurisdictions, as shown in our database, the **_NZA Editor_**. Two are counties; the rest are municipalities. One, Aguilar, has no zoning.

## step 2: iDENTIFY ZONING DISTRICTS

For jurisdictions with zoning, our team obtains zoning texts and maps and sifts through them to identify **_zoning districts_**, each distinctively regulating allowed uses, structures, and lots.

### WE’VE LOGGED 102,000 DISTRICTS — and counting.

We classify districts as:

- Primarily Residential

- Mixed with Residential

- Nonresidential


Some districts are **_overlays_**, which lay atop **_base districts_**. Some districts exist only in the text — we log these districts as **_unmapped_**.

Here are all 19 districts of Galveston, Texas, as shown in the NZA Editor. They include commercial, residential, and industrial uses; a conservation overlay; and an unmapped district.

![Screenshot of a table listing various zip code areas with details on land use types, GIS data upload status, and whether the areas are mixed with residential, nonresidential, or primarily residential.](https://images.squarespace-cdn.com/content/v1/623b33ac24392268745c48d9/3cd2fa10-4903-40e6-b755-9b3c52cd4cbe/Screenshot+2025-04-12+at+8.38.18%E2%80%AFPM.png)

## step 3A: Analyze zoning texts

Our zoning text analysts then convert texts to PDFs, make them searchable, and upload them to the NZA Editor.

### we’ve READ 1,110,000 pages of codes.

As we read… and read… and read… we extract key text datapoints following the NZA’s standard methodology. These data points include:

- Types of residential uses allowed

- Procedures for permitting housing

- Specific lot development requirements, like minimum lot sizes and parking mandates

- Building parameters, like height caps

- Permissions for accessory dwelling units, planned residential developments, and mobile home

## step 3B: CLEAN ZONING MAPS

Meanwhile, our geospatial analysts upload zoning maps — sometimes static PDFs or graphics, sometimes GIS shapefiles — to the NZA Editor.

For maps provided in static format, analysts manually digitize the files. For maps provided in GIS format, analysts redraw misdrawn layers (including unintentional overlaps and leftover “slivers”), remove outdated districts, populate missing overlays, and deal with boundary disputes.

Their last step is “punching out” protected lands, water, rights-of-way, and Tribal lands using comprehensive national layers.

### We’ve mapped the zoning conditions for over 1.36 billion acres of land.

Here is the Albuquerque, New Mexico, zoning map in its native GIS format. The city has 52 zoning districts, with 1 unmapped.

![Color-coded zoning map of Albuquerque, New Mexico, showing different land use districts with a layered legend on the right.](https://images.squarespace-cdn.com/content/v1/623b33ac24392268745c48d9/e0098ef1-63b1-493a-a8ed-7cab6205bf8f/Screenshot+2025-04-12+at+8.17.28%E2%80%AFPM.png)

## step 4: MERGE & PUBLISH

After textual and geospatial analysis for a zoning district is completed, analysts will send the district through our internal review process. After review and approval of all districts in a jurisdiction, and an overall quality control check, we publish the jurisdiction to the [online map](https://www.zoningatlas.org/atlas).

### A FOOTNOTE ON MACHINE LEARNING

[![Cover page of a research report titled 'Automating Zoning Data Collection' with a black background and colorful intersecting lines, and the Urban Institute logo at the bottom.](https://images.squarespace-cdn.com/content/v1/623b33ac24392268745c48d9/a18d7727-b980-425d-997d-6b4b3d91278c/Screenshot+2025-04-09+at+11.41.30%E2%80%AFPM.png)](https://www.urban.org/sites/default/files/2023-02/Automating%20Zoning%20Data%20Collection.pdf)

Could machine learning accelerate completion of the NZA, you ask?

Not the code reading part! Algorithms simply cannot (yet) understand the nuances of lengthy, complex zoning codes to the level of accuracy we require. (We’ve tried since 2022; see this co-authored report with Urban Institute.) **_Anyone who tells you otherwise doesn’t understand zoning._**

However, we are seeking funding to explore machine learning applications for web scraping for code updates and for faster GIS drawing and cleaning.
